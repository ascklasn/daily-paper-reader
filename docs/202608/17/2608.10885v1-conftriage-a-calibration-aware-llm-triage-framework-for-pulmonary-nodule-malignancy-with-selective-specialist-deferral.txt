# ConfTriage: A Calibration-Aware LLM Triage Framework for Pulmonary Nodule Malignancy with Selective Specialist Deferral 

Md Rabiul Islam ∗, Samir Abdaljalil ∗, Erchin Serpedin, Fellow, IEEE , and Hasan Kurban 

Abstract —Pulmonary nodule malignancy prediction typically depends on image-trained specialist deep learning (DL) models that require substantial annotated imaging data and task-specific training. We investigate whether a generalist large language model (LLM), reading only a faithful natural–language rendering of standard nodule attributes, can serve as a calibrated triage layer. To address this question, we proceed in three steps. (1) Framework: We propose ConfTriage, a confidence–calibrated method built on three pillars: language as the modality (the LLM consumes structured–to–language input rather than raw pixels), calibration as the safety mechanism (verbalized confidences are transformed in logit space and Platt–scaled), and a selective specialist DL backstop for low–confidence cases. (2) Theory: We prove two guarantees: a finite–sample combined–error bound that yields an explicit per–threshold operational certificate (Theorem 1); an oracle inequality showing that excess risk over the Bayes–optimal deferral classifier is controlled by the L1 calibration error of the LLM probability, formally tying calibration to triage optimality (Theorem 2). (3) Evidence: A controlled seven–way input ablation across five frontier LLMs (Gemini–3.1–Flash–Lite, Mistral–Large, Qwen–2.5–72B, Claude–3.5–Haiku, GPT–4o–mini) on LIDC–IDRI shows that natural–language descriptions dominate the diagnostic signal, that low–level image statistics are essentially diagnostically vacuous. The proposed ConfTriage achieved an F1 score of 88.22% and an AUC of 0.92, while resolving 76.5% of cases using zero-shot LLM inference alone and referring only the remaining uncertain cases to the specialist DL backstop. These results demonstrate that clinically meaningful diagnostic information can be effectively captured through structured radiological descriptions and leveraged by calibrated LLMs for selective referral. More broadly, the proposed framework suggests a practical pathway for combining generalist LLM prediction with specialist artificial intelligence (AI) models in medical decision-support systems. The source code of ConfTriage is publicly available at github.com/rabiul-ai/ConfTriage. 

Index Terms —confidence calibration, clinical decision support, lung nodule classification, Platt scaling, triage, zero–shot diagnosis. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Low–dose computed tomography (CT) screening for lung cancer detects vast numbers of pulmonary nodules, the majority of which are benign [1]. Triaging these nodules into those that require specialist follow–up and those that do not is a high–volume, time–sensitive task that creates substantial workload for thoracic radiologists. Generalist LLMs offer a complementary capability: they consume structured and free–text descriptions of a finding and return both a diagnosis and a rationale in language. A recent work [5] has shown that GPT–4o can reach an average accuracy of 0.88 on longitudinal lung–nodule malignancy prediction, hinting that the linguistic affordances of LLMs may be useful for triage. Whether such systems are reliable enough to deploy, what input modality actually drives their predictions, and whether their confidences can be calibrated for safe operational use remain open questions. 

The gap: For pulmonary nodule malignancy classification, specialized supervised convolutional neural network (CNN) [2]– [4] and vision transformer (ViT) [6] pipelines have achieved high accuracy but require task-specific training and offer limited linguistic interpretability. Vision–language pipelines built on contrastive language–image pretraining (CLIP) and segment anything model (SAM) variants [7], [8] reach an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of approximately 0.90 on the National Lung Screening Trial cohort but degrade under distribution shift to LUNGx and provide no controlled disentanglement of input–modality contributions. Generalist LLM evaluations in radiology [5], [9]–[11] report headline accuracy figures from single models on heterogeneous tasks, without isolating which information channel carries the signal, without producing a calibrated triage policy, and without comparing across vendors on a single benchmark with rigorous statistical machinery. Practitioners deciding whether to use an LLM as a triage layer therefore have neither (i) a controlled measurement of how much of the signal is linguistic versus visual, nor (ii) a deployable method that turns LLM confidence into a defensible deferral policy with formal guarantees. 

The insight: The proposed ConfTriage is a methodolog-ical framework rather than a single model. Two empirical observations and one theoretical observation, taken together, motivate a structured framework for how clinical AI for triage might be organized in the LLM era. First (empirical), standard radiology workflow already produces a small set of structured nodule descriptors (subtlety, internal structure, calcification, sphericity, margin, lobulation, spiculation, texture). If those descriptors carry most of the diagnostic signal, then a generalist LLM that operates on a faithful language rendering of them does not need to consume the CT volume directly. Second arXiv:2608.10885v1 [cs.CV] 11 Aug 2026 2

(empirical), this property is not specific to a single vendor: if it holds for one frontier LLM, it appears to hold across multiple frontier LLMs from independent providers, which is operationally important because deployments cannot lock to a single closed model. Third (theoretical), a calibrated LLM probability is not just a presentation aid; it is the formal object that makes a deferral policy near–optimal in a precise sense (Theorem 2). Together, these observations support a framework with three pillars: (P1) language as the modality (the LLM consumes a structured–to–language rendering rather than raw pixels); (P2) calibration as the safety mechanism (verbalized confidences are transformed in logit space and Platt–scaled); and (P3) a selective specialist backstop as the formal certificate (low–confidence cases route to a specialist DL classifier, with provable bounds on combined error). All three pillars are empirically falsifiable. In this paper, we test P1 directly through the controlled input ablation. We develop the theoretical scaffolding for P2 and P3 (Theorems 1, 2). The resulting combined operating curve, obtained through Platt scaling on Dcal and a deferral–threshold sweep, extends the uncertain–case rejection framework of Certain-Net [12] into a calibrated selective–prediction setting. 

Approach: To support the framework empirically and theoretically, we (i) construct a deterministic input–construction function ϕ that produces seven input regimes spanning ‘ra-diology attributes only,’ ‘language description only,’ ‘image statistics only,’ and four combinations, and evaluate five frontier LLMs from independent vendors on the LIDC–IDRI benchmark under each regime; (ii) introduce ConfTriage, a confidence– calibrated triage framework whose two theorems supply, respectively, an operational error budget and a calibration– to–optimality bridge; and (iii) perform attribute–redaction and synthetic–corruption controls together with an evaluation against leave–one–out (LOO) consensus labels derived from the available LIDC–IDRI reader annotations, thereby quanti-fying model performance relative to expert-reader variability (Section VI-F). All analyses use only publicly available de– identified data; statistical testing uses paired bootstrap with Benjamini–Hochberg false–discovery–rate (FDR) control across all reported comparisons. Overall, the major contributions of our research are as follows. 1) Method: We articulate a three–pillar methodological frame-work for clinical triage with generalist LLMs (language as modality, calibration as safety mechanism, selective backstop as certificate) and instantiate it as ConfTriage, a confidence–calibrated method using logit–space of ver-balized confidences, Platt scaling on a held–out fold, and threshold–based deferral to a specialist DL backstop (Section III, Algorithm 2). 2) Formal guarantees: We prove two theoretical results supporting the proposed framework. Theorem 1 pro-vides a finite–sample combined–error bound that yields a per–threshold operational certificate. Theorem 2 establishes an oracle inequality showing that the excess risk of ConfTriage relative to the Bayes–optimal deferral classifier is controlled by the L1 calibration error of the LLM probability, formally connecting probability calibration to triage optimality. 3) Empirical findings: A controlled seven–way input abla-tion across five frontier LLMs on LIDC–IDRI demon-strates that natural–language descriptions dominate the LLM diagnostic signal, that image–statistics inputs are essentially diagnostically vacuous, and that a small subset of standard LIDC–IDRI attributes localizes most of the gap. Significance is assessed using paired bootstrap with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (Section VI). 4) Reliability and robustness evaluation: Comprehensive evalu-ations are performed to assess the reliability of ConfTriage through LOO reader-consensus evaluation and calibration analysis, while robustness of the language-based diagnostic signal is examined through synthetic corruption controls, description-source ablation, and prompt-temperature sensi-tivity studies. 

Key Distinctions: The closest prior work [5] evaluates GPT–4o on longitudinal CT for nodule assessment and reports 0.88 average accuracy. Our contribution differs along three specific axes: (i) we propose a named, deployable triage method with calibration, deferral, and finite–sample guarantees, rather than reporting raw model accuracy; (ii) we perform a controlled seven–way input ablation that disentangles which input channel drives the LLM signal, which Mao et al. did not attempt; and (iii) we evaluate five vendors rather than one, which is operationally relevant because deployments cannot lock to a single closed model.

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Specialized DL for Nodule Classification 

MV–KBC [2] decomposes a 3D nodule into nine fixed views and integrates appearance, voxel, and shape submodels through ResNet50 backbones; on LIDC–IDRI it reaches 91.60% accu-racy and AUC of 0.957. SDTL [3] couples nodule–vs–tissue pretraining with iterative feature–matching pseudo–labeling. Vi-sual–attention–driven self–supervised transfer learning [4] and LoRA adaptation of pretrained large vision models [6] continue this line and report further accuracy gains. More recently, Gu-nawan et al. [13] proposed a multistage preprocessing pipeline combining 3D filtering with a modified segmentation network, reporting high classification accuracy through extensive image preprocessing and supervised CNN training. These methods are accurate but each requires nontrivial image–level training and does not produce native linguistic interfaces for clinician auditing. Our approach is operationally distinct: we test how far a generalist LLM operating on structured–to–language input can go and use specialized DL only as a backstop for low–confidence cases. 

B. Vision–Language Pipelines and LLM Evaluation 

Lung–CADex [7] couples MedSAM zero–shot segmentation with CLIP prefix tuning and reports detection sensitivity of 0.86 on LIDC–IDRI compared with 0.76 for fully supervised baselines, with external evaluation on LUNGx. The seman-tic–guided vision–language model (VLM) of [8] aligns CT image features with sentence–level radiologist semantic features generated via Gemini and reaches AUC 0.901 on the NLST test split. Both methods integrate vision and language but do not isolate the contribution of language alone, do not emit calibrated confidences, and do not implement a deferral policy. Few studies [14], [15] evaluate the LLMs in Radiology. For instances, Brin et al. [9] evaluate GPT–4V on 230 emer-gency–room images and report pathology–recognition accuracy of 35.2%. Sonoda et al. [10] compare GPT–4o, Claude 3 Opus, and Gemini 1.5 Pro on 324 ‘Diagnosis Please’ cases. Hu et al. [11] benchmark GPT–5 against GPT–4o on VQA–RAD, SLAKE, and a curated medical–physics multiple–choice set. Mao et al. [5] provide the most directly relevant prior result: GPT–4o reaches 0.88 average accuracy on longitudinal nodule malignancy assessment over 647 patients. None of these papers performs a controlled multi-modality ablation, packages the results as a calibrated triage method with formal guarantees, or provides multi-vendor comparisons under FDR-corrected statistical testing on a single nodule benchmark. 

C. Selective Classification and Learning to Defer 

ConfTriage is, mathematically, a selective classifier with an external backstop. The selective classification framework of Geifman and El–Yaniv [16] formalizes coverage–risk trade–offs for classifiers that abstain; the learning–to–defer framework of Madras et al. [17] extends this to systems that route to a downstream expert. We are not aware of a prior instantiation of either framework for an LLM–plus–specialist hybrid in clinical triage. A. Problem Formulation 

Let a single lung nodule case be the tuple x = ( v, a, s ) ∈ X ,where v ∈ RH×W ×D is the cropped 3D CT volume centered at the nodule, a ∈ Rk is a vector of k structured radiology attributes, and s is a deterministic natural–language description of the nodule. We use the eight standard LIDC–IDRI nod-ule–characteristic attributes ( k = 8 ): subtlety, internal structure, calcification, sphericity, margin, lobulation, spiculation, and texture. In addition, nodule diameter ( d) and estimated anatom-ical position derived from the nodule centroid are included. Let 

y ∈ { 0, 1} denote the binary benign–versus–malignant target, and let P denote the joint distribution over (x, y ) induced by the source population. We seek a function that, given x,outputs either a calibrated probability estimate ˆp ∈ [0 , 1] of the Bayes posterior η(x) = Pr P [y = 1 | x] or a deferral signal handing the case to a specialist DL classifier fDL .

A. Dataset 

The LIDC–IDRI [1] is the primary benchmark that was utilized for conducting the research. The full database contains 1018 thoracic CT scans with lesions marked as a nodule by at least one thoracic radiologist. Following standard practice [2]–[4], we exclude nodules with diameter ≤ 3 mm, and we exclude nodules with median malignancy rating equal to 3 because of irreducible diagnostic ambiguity. After these inclusion criteria, 955 nodules are retained (427 benign, 528 malignant), partitioned into five patient–wise cross–validation folds. We partitioned the labeled dataset D = {(xi, y i)}Ni=1 using patient-level five-fold cross-validation to prevent patient-level leakage. For each iteration, fold i was used as the held-out test set Dtest , while fold (i + 1) mod 5 served as the calibration set 

Dcal for fitting the Platt-scaling parameters and determining the operating threshold. The learned calibration model was then applied only to the corresponding test fold, ensuring that each sample received exactly one out-of-fold calibrated probability. 

B. Reporting Protocol 

For all models and ablation experiments, we report accuracy (Acc.), F1 score, and AUC. In addition, the expected calibration error (ECE), Brier score, sensitivity (Sen.), specificity (Spe.), and Cohen’s kappa ( κ) are reported where relevant. All point estimates are accompanied by 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals computed from 300 patient-level bootstrap resamples of Dtest , where resampling with replacement at the patient level preserves the dependence structure introduced by multiple nodules per patient. Pairwise testing under FDR control: Pairwise model com-parisons under a fixed ablation use the paired bootstrap on AUC at the patient level; the resulting p–values are subjected to Benjamini–Hochberg false–discovery–rate correction [20] across all reported comparisons. Tables IV use 300 bootstrap resamples for these pairwise tests. Because this bootstrap budget implies a minimum attainable raw p-value of approximately 

1/(300+1) ≈ 0.0033 , we report significance as ∗∗ for q < 0.01 

and ∗ for q < 0.05 .VI. R ESULTS AND ANALYSIS

VII. D ISCUSSION 

A. Operational Implication 

The headline finding is not that LLMs “beat” specialist DL; the finding is that, when a structured radiology workflow already produces nodule attributes, an LLM operating on a faithful language rendering of those attributes produces predictions of clinically meaningful quality at zero training cost and modest API cost. ConfTriage extends this from a measurement to a methodological framework: the calibrated probability and the deferral threshold turn LLM confidence into a defensible routing decision, and Theorem 1 provides a finite–sample guarantee on the resulting combined error. We position the present paper as a methodological contribution validated on public benchmarks; prospective clinical validation is the natural follow–on study and is enabled by the public release. 

B. Language vs Image Information 1) Why Language Dominates: Two non–exclusive explana-tions for the empirical discovery in Section VI-B are consistent with our results. First, generalist LLMs are trained on enormous corpora of medical text in which malignancy correlates with specific terms ( spiculated , lobulated , ground–glass ); a faithful language rendering of attributes therefore recovers a strong prior. Second, the structured attributes themselves carry most of the diagnostic signal in LIDC–IDRI, and language is simply the most efficient packaging the LLM can consume. The synthetic–corruption controls in Section VI-B are designed to disambiguate these two: if (a) negation collapses AUC and (b) paraphrase preserves it, the LLM is genuinely consuming semantic content, not just exploiting trigger terms. 

2) Why Image–Statistics Inputs Fail: Low–level histogram and GLCM statistics are not a representation that generalist LLMs were trained to interpret in a clinical sense. The result is consistent with a broader theme in the literature: GPT–4V and similar models do well on modality and anatomy recognition but poorly on lesion–level pathology identification [9]. Our paper does not contradict the value of vision–language pipelines that align CT features to language [7], [8]; it shows instead that, when structured language input is available, generalist LLMs achieve much of the benefit without the alignment training. 

C. What the Theory Adds 

The two theorems in Section IV are not deep mathematical contributions; they are honest applications of classical tools to the specific framework. Their value is operational. Theorem 1 converts vague claims (“ConfTriage seems to work”) into auditable statements (“the combined error is at most 25 .6% 

with probability 0.95 ”) that a clinical safety officer can read 13 

and verify. Theorem 2 is the more conceptually interesting result: it shows that calibration (which is observable through ECE) controls the gap to the Bayes–optimal deferral classifier. The empirical results support this prediction: Platt calibration reduced ECE by approximately 50%, increased LLM decision coverage from 65.5% to 76.5%. Without Theorem 2, the calibration step in ConfTriage would be a heuristic aimed at producing more reliable confidence estimates; with it, calibration becomes a directly measurable mechanism for improving routing decisions and reducing triage suboptimality. 

D. Limitations 

1) LIDC–IDRI label subjectivity: The malignancy labels in LIDC–IDRI are based on radiologist assessments rather than pathology–confirmed diagnoses. Future work should validate the proposed framework on pathology–proven datasets to assess its performance against definitive clinical ground truth. 2) No prospective clinical validation: This study presents a methodological framework evaluated on public benchmarks. We do not claim prospective deployment performance and did not conduct a recruited reader study. While the LIDC–IDRI radiologist annotations provide a bench-mark human reference, prospective external validation, multi–institution reader studies, workflow–integrated evalu-ation, and local calibration audits remain necessary before clinical deployment. 3) Limited multimodal public datasets: Public datasets provid-ing both CT images and expert textual descriptions for lung nodule malignancy assessment are scarce. Consequently, this study is limited to LIDC–IDRI, where structured textual descriptions were generated. 

E. Design Recommendations for AI Developers 

Three concrete recommendations follow from the present results, each tied to a specific finding rather than to the framework as a whole. 

Design Recommendations for AI Developers  

> R1

Use language input, not raw–image statistics, when invoking a generalist LLM as a triage classifier. 

Our seven–way ablation (Table III) shows that across five vendors, low–level image statistics deliver near–chance discrimination, while a deterministic natural–language rendering of standard radiology attributes routinely yields AUC ≥ 0.79 and reaches AUC = 0.907 for the strongest model. The evidence does not support feeding raw histogram or GLCM statistics as a substitute for a language description.  

> R2

When deploying, calibrate first, then threshold. 

Theorem 2 ties ConfTriage’s excess risk to the L1

calibration error of the LLM probability. In practice this means: hold out a calibration fold, fit Platt scaling on the verbalized logit, then sweep the deferral threshold under a sensitivity–floor constraint (we use 

Sens ≥ 0.95 ). Skipping the calibration step turns the deferral threshold into a heuristic with no formal guarantee.  

> R3

Pair the LLM with a backstop that itself emits a calibrated rejection signal. 

The Certain–Net backstop’s MC–dropout uncertainty separates correct from incorrect predictions; this is the empirical precondition that Theorem 1’s certificate relies on. A backstop without an internal calibration audit (e.g., a single deterministic CNN with no uncertainty estimate) does not provide the same operational guarantee. 

F. Broader Impact 

A triage system that defers hard cases to a specialist DL backstop is operationally appealing because it makes the failure mode explicit (low confidence → deferral) rather than hidden. This contrasts with a single–model classifier whose miscalibration is invisible at use time. We caution that linguistic outputs from LLMs can be persuasive even when wrong, and any clinical deployment must include a verbalized–confidence audit and a deferral path back to a human reader. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced ConfTriage, a confidence-calibrated triage framework that combines a generalist LLM with a selective specialist DL backstop for pulmonary nodule malignancy prediction. ConfTriage achieved an F1 score of 0.882 while resolving 76.5% of cases through zero-shot LLM inference alone. These findings suggest that structured radiological information expressed in natural language can effectively support LLM-based clinical triage. Ultimately, ConfTriage demonstrates that confidence-aware selective referral provides an effective mechanism for leveraging the complementary strengths of generalist LLMs and specialist AI models in clinical decision support.
