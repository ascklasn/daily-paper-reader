ABSTRACT 19 

20 

Selection of regions of interest (ROIs) is often a crucial step in spatial molecular profiling and many 21 

pathology tasks , with substantial implications for  research  reproducibility and biological interpretability .22 

To provide a reproducible and adaptive framework for AI -guided ROI sele ction, w e developed a 23 

modular generalist -specialist solution across spatial profiling platforms . In a cohort co mprising 55 tumor 24 

types from 160 tissue donors profiled using NanoString Digital Spatial Profiling and multiplex 25 

immunofluorescen ce , we first established a protein -profiling reference atlas capturing compartment -26 

specific immune, checkpoint, stromal, and proliferation patterns. We then developed an AI Specialist 27 

Task -Oriented Model for ROI Selection (ASTROS ) and tested comprehensive benchmark s considering 28 

specialist -only (ASTROS), generalist -only (PLIP/GFM), and hybrid generalist -specialist strategies, 29 

showing that the latter provides a balanced tradeoff across slide -level signal preservation, pathologist -30 

reference concordance, within -slide placement consistency, and large -slide computational efficiency .31 

We further demonstrate d the feasibility of virtual staining for ROI preview and modular ROI placement 32 

for other spatial omics technologies, Visium and Visium HD workflows. Together, these results support 33 

our proposed framework to enable ROI selection responding to unmet needs for reducing inter -rater 34 

variability, reproducibility , and versati lity in spatial profiling experiments .35

INTRODUCTION 37 

Tumor tissue sections are essential snapshots of neoplasm evolution whose intricacies underpin 38 

cancer biology and clinical outcome 1. Yet across pathology, genomics, and spatial omics, the biology of 39 

a whole tissue sect io n is rarely accessible ; surgical resections can span several centimeters (up to 3.7 40 

× 2.4 cm 2 per FFPE cassette), whereas spatial profiling platforms capture only small fractions of that 41 

area per experiment in the order of 660 × 785 μm ² for NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling 42 

(DSP) 2, 800 × 800 μm² for multiplexed ion beam imaging (MIBI) 4, and up to 6.5 × 6.5 mm² or 11 × 11 43 

mm² area for 10x Genomics Visium and Xenium platforms 3, respectively. Constrained capture areas, 44 

assay cost, and limited throughput therefore force selecting one or a few representative regions of 45 

interest (ROIs) from large heterogeneous tissue . This step underpins downstream analyses including 46 

tumor microenvironment (TME) characterization 5, detection of immune infiltration, and quantification of 47 

immune checkpoint status 6, cancer grading 7, biomarker validation 8,9 , and diagnostic assessments 10 ,48 

and at the functional level can yield new insights into immunotherapy responses 11,12 , therefore, the 49 

choice of ROI directly affects study outcomes, reproducibility, and scientific validity. Yet ROI selection 50 

has traditionally been manual and expert dependent, leaving it prone to interrater variability 13 ,51 

susceptible to biases in how the whole tissue is represented 14 –17 , and poorly scalable amid a growing 52 

pathologist workforce shortage 18 . Reproducible, representative ROI selection is therefore a central 53 

bottleneck for large -scale spatial profiling  compounded by the fact that capture areas, assays and the 54 

biological questions the y serve may diff er across platforms, so no single, fixed selection rule transfers 55 

cleanly between ROI selection strategies .56 

Automated approache s promise to make ROI selection more reproducible by streamlining complex 57 

workflows and enabling large -scale, multi -center studies. Several studies have accordingly examined 58 

how tissue sampling represent s the tumor heterogeneity. For example , Sun et al. 5 derived a minimum 59 

area required to represent whole -slide images (WSIs) from the rank correlation between ROIs and 60 

WSIs, in line with established sampling principles for whole -slide tissues 16,17,20 ; and, more recently , Bost 61 

et al. 21 developed a systematic framework for determining the number and size of ROIs needed to 62 

accurately identify all cell phenotypes in sit u in multiplexed images. In parallel, the accelerated progress 63 

of artificial intelligence (AI) in computational pathology has motivated classification and detection -based 64 

models for ROI selection 22 –24 , including its formulation as a segmentation task through boundary update 65 

and coar se-to -fine super -pixel refin ement 25 . Accounting for representativeness, Shen et al. 26 localized 66 

representative patches using a pathology deformable con ditio nal random field with a convolutional 67 

neural network backbone , Rezk et al. 27 generated discriminative samples that recapitulate the 68 

population distribution via formal concept analysis, and Hossain et al. 28 implemented a vision 69 

transformer for ROI detection in HER2 scoring of breast cancer. Despite these significant efforts, two 70 

major issues remain. Firstly , a systematic criterion has yet to be widely adopted with a clear goal, e.g., 71 

reducing inter -rater variability , and secondly, most of these specialist models are built for a single 72 

pred efined task and cannot be readily adapted to new requirements, tissues, or platforms .73 

Recently popularized,  generalist foundational models (GFMs) trained on a diverse corpus leverage 74 

broad contextual understanding and transfer learning promise to be adapt ive across tasks 29 -31 . At the 75 

other end of the generalist -specialist continuum, specialist task -oriented models (STMs) widely 76 

employed in healthcare currently achieve strong performance on narrowly defined problems 32 , but with 77 

limited generalizability . STMs are typically fine -tuned for precise pathology task s or disease types such 78 

as Gleason grading in prostate cancer 33 . Thus far, GFMs and STMs have largely been built and 79 

deployed as standalone pipelines rather than interoperable modules which would have their 80 

complementary strengths 34 . Therefore, h arnessing both approaches synerg istically is a promising 81

Building on insights from manual ROIs in the DSP cohort , we then sought to develop a biology -oriented 204 

automated workflow using a hybrid generalist -specialist framework and enable reproducible, large -205 

scale analysis across diverse tumor types. To automate the process of ROI selection for DSP, we 206 

utilized the manual RO Is for this as say retrospectively accessed and devised a three -stage (Fig. 3a ),207

(Fig. 3b ), and culminating in ROI selection by ASTROS, our AI Specialist Task -oriented model for ROI 209 

Selection . We designed an extensive comparison of approaches ( Fig. 3c ) to assess the advantages of 210 

using a synergistic modular gener ali st-specialist approach rather than its individual components 211 

standalone .212

The second stage established the basis of representativity , w e identified which and how many 227

number of ROIs per sample (N = 6 ) according to t hat proportion , maintaining the WSI representativity 231 

across immune categories. Next , to define which grids from a WSI would pass to stage 3 , we compared 232

DISCUSSION 480 

The automation of ROI selection for tissue assessment has been identified as one of the AI applications 481 

that will be most likely used by pathology labs by 203 043 . Ideally, automation should enable a consistent 482 

and reproducible captur e of relevant biological patterns in tissues , while maintaining whole -tissue 483 

section representativity and adaptab il ity across diverse tissue profiling platforms and project needs .484 

Previous works 22 –24,27,28,44 have demonstrat ed how AI -based approaches can contribute to ROI 485 

selectio n through single -model approaches, such as convolutional neural networks or vision 486 

transformers. However, t he deployment of niche computational pathology models hinder s swift 487 

adaptation s to heterogeneous histological data . Foundation models hav e emerged as a potential 488 

solution to bridge the gap 29 . New computational frameworks are needed to leverage the powerful 489 

con te xtual capacities of foundational m od els while inc orporat ing the task -oriented performance of 490 

specialist models to facilitate reproducible scientific discoveries . In this work , we show ed the feasibility 491 

of a generalist -speci alist modular integration for ROI se lec tion as an explainable and adaptive 492 

approach , expeditiously applicable to different image modalities, research questions, and spatial omics 493 

assays .494 

We defined representativity, flexibility, and scalability in a task -specific manner. Representativity refers 495 

to preservation of WSI and manual ROIs histological features relevant to the automated ROI selection .496 

Flexibility accounts for the ability to combine generalist tissue -similarity filtering with specialist ROI 497 

localization in a modular fashion. Finally, scalability refers to the ability to apply this workflow across 498 

large WSIs with measurable runtime and annotation -efficiency tradeoffs. For multiplex 499 

immunofluorescence, w e described a three -stage process that leverages published foundational 500 

models and a custom -designed STM, ASTROS, which emulates human selection , capturing broader 501 

patterns across samples, followed by targeted optimization and selection of specific ROI s. While 502 

ASTROS was trained with extensive manual annotations, a s we demonstrated, a relatively small 503 

manual reference set may be sufficient due to the contextual information -processing capability and the 504 

growing performance of GFMs 29 . This will depend on the complexity of the ROI selection criteria .505 

Alternatively, t o reduce reliance on extensive annotations , less supervised approaches and contrastive 506 

learning may be introduced or morphology to omics translations 45 with the associated challenges of 507 

representing morphology and omics in a shared embedding space 46 . Although subjectivity can be 508 

introduced through manual annotations used for training , it remains essential to validate expert -domain 509 

annotations and anchor the selection on established protocols to ensure reliability . As we demonstrated 510 

in this study, manual selection of ROIs guided by community -established guidelines for immune 511 

presence in tumor tissues 36 help s to capture immunoproteomic activation patterns. Through automation 512 

of this process, our workflow can reduce labor , particularly for double ROI selection (first in H&E and 513 

then on mIF ), while accounting for the variability and complexit y across tumor types . The value of a514 

modular generalist –specialist framework lies , henceforth, in allowing these objectives to be combined, 515 

tuned, and extended without constraining the ROI selection task into a single rigid model. 516 

We primarily focused on the analysis of the immune -based NanoString ’s GeoMx DSP platform ;517 

however, our framework can be easily tailored to diverse scenarios due to its modular layout . For 518 

example, w e expanded the application for ROI selection for the Visium platform s, which showcased the 519 

application to other tissue compartments as an explainable approach while keeping the expert in the 520

One aspect that partially escaped our evaluation is the effect of sample size . This is particularly 533 

relevant when multiple candidate ROIs are present in a tissue . Sampling theory suggests that the 534 

precision of estimating population parameters increases asymptotically with sample size , as 535 

demonstrated in ROI selection 5,21 and cohort -level simulations 49 .Our results point in the same direction .536 

In recent efforts, Baker et al. 50 proposed a power analysis framework that explores the design of spatial 537 

molecular assays by incorporating parametrization based on cellular distribution, with applications such 538 

as the detection of cell -cell adjacencies as a function of sampling effort. For spatial pathomics assays , it 539 

is challenging to test the effect of sample size ( i.e. , the number of ROIs) on omics patterns due to the 540

challenges could be exacerbated during the synthetic immunofluorescence stage, as H&E and mIF 555

cases with complex and infrequent morpholog ies , a fine -tuned version could be deployed together with 557

a robust , modular AI -Pathology ecosystem ca n successfully leverage their strengths and enable their 562

advance s scientific reproducibility , improve s data analysis scalability , and has a profound impact on 564 

clinical utility in domains such as biomarker discovery and deployment by eliminating technical noise 565

Scenarios for ROI selection in the generalist -specialist framework 941 

To illustrate some of the potential use cases, we develop three scenarios that demonstrate how GFM 942 

and STM can be used standalone or both integrated with a pathologist’s input to give a streamlined 943 

solution to common needs. Although these represent some possible uses, the expanding modularity 944 

can accommodate a different number of models tailored to other applications and the specific goals of a 945 

study. 946

Scenario 1: capture representative tumor areas : The goal of this scenario was to showcase GFM usage 952 

for selecting ROIs based on a set of manually chosen ROIs by training a minimalist morphology -based 953 

ROI selection model that learns the embedding -space signature of manually selected ROIs. This was to 954 

fulfill a common need to select ROIs capturing representative tumor areas while maximizing viable 955 

tissue coverage within the ROI. 956 

To develop this scenario, the model was trained with ROIs manually selected for LUNG, CCA, 957 

and UTUC. Although diverse in morphology, this set shared a minimal set of features, such as viable 958 

tumor tissue. The tissue was segmented and tiled (256 × 256 pixels 2) to extract tile -level embeddings. 959 

For each tile, a binary label was assigned based on whether its center was within the manual ROI 960 

(positive) or fell outside (negative). To compensate for imbalanced datasets, negative non -ROI tiles are 961 

randomly downsampled to match the number of positive ROI tiles. To train the ROI -tile model, 962 

embeddings were normalized using StandardScaler (Python: scikit -learn), and a Logistic Regression 963 

model was trained to classify ROI vs non -ROI tiles. An 80/20 train -validation split is applied for 964 

evaluation. During training, the primary evaluation metric was the area under the receiver operator 965 

curve (AUC). When evaluated on the independent validation set, the model achieved an area under the 966 

ROC curve (AUC) of 0.963, reflecting excellent discriminative ability. The ROC profile indicated that the 967 

classifier maintained high sensitivity across a broad range of specificity levels, suggesting that the 968 

combination of balanced sampling, standardized features, and a regularized logistic regression model 969 

produced a robust and reliable predictor. 970 

To assess the scenario, we employed a diverse set of metrics to both visually and quantitatively 971 

evaluate the prediction. Each tile received a positive -classification probability, resulting in a probability 972 

map over the entire slide, capturing the spatial distribution of “ROI -likeness”. To reach an optimized 973 

ROI selection, a rectangular window matching the size of the training ROIs was slid over the WSI. At 974 

each iteration, the average ROI probability of the tiles inside the moving window was calculated. The 975 

window with the highest average ROI probability was selected as the predicted ROI, identifying the 976 

most viable region in terms of morphology. The tile probabilities were visualized as a heatmap to 977 

provide an intuitive, spatial overview of where ROI -like morphology is concentrated. Finally, we 978 

calculated the intersection over union score (IOU) to measure the overlap between the predicted ROI 979 

and the manual annotation. 980

requirements for including or excluding certain tissue components, such as necrosis. For this purpose, 982

necrosis ratio in ROIs, identifying viable regions suitable for spatial transcriptomics while harnessing the 984 

performance and explainability of STMs. 985

Scenario 3: Human -AI collaboration: A final scenario entailed the interaction of GFM, STM, and the 997 

pathologist’s input in the form of a manually segmented mask. This scenario can be useful in cases 998 

when ROI needs to be directed to a specific region of the tissue, coarsely defined by manual input ( e.g., 999 

tumor bed). We aimed to design a cascaded pipeline that used pathologist -provided tumor masks to 1000

annotations to anchor the model to the tumor region, an STM (TMESegformer) for TME segmentation 1002

morphological patterns for final ROI selection. 1004

Then, this region was intersected with the TMESegformer prediction mask. Tissue components, 1006 

including necrosis, tumor, immune, and stromal components, were identified at the pixel level. A filter 1007
